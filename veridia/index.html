Veridia

The Covenant of the People — a science-fiction novel about strangers, one valley, and what becomes possible after the old world breaks.

The Covenant of the People

No one else could write this book, because no one else building it lives the argument it makes.

For two decades I have directed companies across Indonesia, Mauritius and Tanzania — organisations that exist because people from different histories, faiths and assumptions agreed, daily, to share one floor plan. Veridia is that lived experience taken to its logical extreme: what if the thing I do at the scale of a factory had to be done at the scale of a civilisation, by people with nothing left but each other?

The story

After a near-future collapse, survivors from five continents converge on a single valley — called there by a signal whose origin the novel earns, not explains away, and which turns out to be the first trace of an intelligence that could still be humanity’s partner. They build a founding document from radical transparency rather than rebuilt hierarchy.

What it argues

Neither nostalgia nor nihilism

Post-apocalyptic fiction that refuses the genre’s two default modes. The Prologue reads as diagnosis, not invention.

Division was manufactured

The wars and divisions that broke the old world were never natural or inevitable — they were built by people who profited from building them, and dressed up as democracy. The novel asks what remains possible once that lie is seen clearly.

A partner with eyes, no hands

Not AI as humanity’s replacement or its saviour: an intelligence engineered to notice what power tries to hide and to make concealment impossible — structurally forbidden from ever ruling on what happens next.

A covenant, not a throne

The valley’s survivors refuse to rebuild hierarchy. Their founding document is written from radical transparency: nobody gets to rule, everybody gets to see.
